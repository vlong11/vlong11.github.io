4. Construct a 95% confidence interval for the variance of heart rate among nonsmokers

# 95% confidence interval for variance of nonsmokers

n = length(nonsmokers)        #count number of nonsmokers
s2 = var(nonsmokers)          #sample variance
df = n - 1                 
alpha = 0.05

# Calculate confidence interval -- Chi-square distribution for one population variance
lower = (df*s2)/qchisq(1-alpha/2, df)        #Lower CI: (n-1)s^2 / large chi-square
upper = (df*s2)/qchisq(alpha/2, df)         #Upper CI: (n-1)s^2 / small chi-square

# Display results
round(c(lower, upper), 4)
## [1]  20.4853 239.2220

Conclusion: We are 95% confident that the true variance of resting heart rates among nonsmokers is between 20.4853 and 239.2220 bpm².

5. Construct a 95% confidence interval for the ratio of variances between light smokers and heavy smokers

n1 = length(light)            #counts light smokers
n2 = length(heavy)            #counts heavy smokers

s1 = var(light)              #sample variance of light smokers
s2 = var(heavy)              #sample variance of heavy smokers

df1 = n1 - 1                 #numerator degrees of freedom
df2 = n2 - 1                 #denominator degrees of freedom

alpha = 0.05
 
# Calculate confidence interval -- F-distribution for Ratio of two population variances         
lower = (s1/s2)/qf(1-alpha/2, df1, df2)     #qf finds F critical value
upper = (s1/s2)/qf(alpha/2, df1, df2)

# Display results
round(c(lower, upper), 4)
## [1] 0.1739 5.8884

Conclusion: We are 95% confident that the true ratio of heart rate variances between light smokers and heavy smokers is between 0.1739 and 5.8884. Since the 95% confidence interval contains 1, there is not enough evidence to conclude that the heart rate variances of light smokers and heavy smokers are different.

6. Hypothesis test for equality of variances between light smokers and heavy smokers

Null hypothesis:

\[ H_0: \sigma_{\text{Light}}^2 = \sigma_{\text{Heavy}}^2 \]

Alternative hypothesis:

\[ H_a: \sigma_{\text{Light}}^2 \neq \sigma_{\text{Heavy}}^2 \]

# Calculate F test statistic
F0 = var(light)/var(heavy)                      #sample variance(light)/sample variance(heavy)

# Calculate two-tailed p-value
p_value = 2 * min(pf(F0, 6, 6),                #pf(F0, 6, 6) find prob. to the left of the F-statistic
                 1 - pf(F0, 6, 6))              #find prob. to the right of the F-statistic
                                                 #min() for smaller tail then double it because is two tailed (2*)

# Display results
round(c(F_statistic = F0, P_value = p_value), 4)
## F_statistic     P_value 
##      1.0118      0.9890

Conclusion: Since the p-value (0.9890) is greater than \(\alpha = 0.05\), we fail to reject \(H_0\).

Final Conclusion: At the 5% significance level, we fail to reject \(H_0\). There is not enough evidence to conclude that the population variances of resting heart rates differ between light smokers and heavy smokers.

8. State the ANOVA hypotheses, report the F-statistic and p-value, and interpret the result.

Null hypothesis:

\[ H_0: \mu_{\text{None}} = \mu_{\text{Light}} = \mu_{\text{Heavy}} \]

Alternative hypothesis:

\[ H_a: \text{At least one population mean differs.} \] Conclusion: The ANOVA test resulted in an F-statistic of 19.62 and a p-value of 0.0000301. Since the p-value is less than \(\alpha = 0.05\), we reject \(H_0\).

Final Conclusion: At the 5% significance level, we reject \(H_0\). There is enough evidence to conclude that at least one smoking group has a different population mean resting heart rate.
